# Friends: invite the people you've played with, no address required

**Status:** designed 2026-09-02, reviewed and revised the same day;
planned 2026-09-05 in
[docs/plans/2026-09-05-invites-and-friends.md](../docs/plans/2026-09-05-invites-and-friends.md).
**Home:** this repo, same reasoning as its parent. This spec layers onto
[2026-09-01-email-invites-and-seat-keys.md](2026-09-01-email-invites-and-seat-keys.md)
and assumes its machinery (reserved seats, invite tokens, the seat key);
nothing here stands without it. It adds **no host contract changes**: the
parent spec's two capabilities are enough, and the friend layer lives
entirely inside `packages/notify` and client UI. It does carry one
prerequisite of its own, named first, because without it two of the three
games cannot take part at all.

## The property being bought

**Inviting someone you've played with is picking a name, not typing an
address.** The system reaches that person through their own registered
notification settings, push or email per their prefs, and the inviter
never sees, needs, or can extract the address. Email invites stay for
first contact with someone the system has never met; friends cover
everyone else, which after the first game night is nearly everyone.

The privacy stance is the point, not a side effect: the game surfaces
show only what a table already shows, the name someone played under.
Addresses live where they already live, in notify's profile records, and
never cross to another player.

## Current state (what "friend" can even be made of)

The only cross-game, cross-room identity anywhere is the notify profile:
`profileId = sha256(playerKey)`, holding channels and prefs
(`packages/notify/records.ts`). The lobby's identity is per-room seats;
its names are per-room roster entries plus the client-side shared
`lobby.name` key. Nothing durable connects "this person in room A" to
"this person in room B" except the profile.

So "players you've played with" must mean: **profiles that were bound to
seats in a room where your profile was also bound.** Who that covers is
wider than it sounds, and narrower than it should be, for reasons worth
being exact about:

- **Wider:** binding is not a notification opt-in. Wordgame's
  `useNotifyBind` fires for every player the moment a room enters the
  playing phase, and `getPlayerKey()` mints a key on first call, so every
  wordgame player with working storage has a profile and gets bound
  whether or not they ever opened the settings panel. The ledger will
  hold everyone you played with; most entries will be unreachable until
  their owner adds a channel. **`reachable` carries the whole load**, and
  the UI has to make that state legible (see the handoff list).
- **Narrower:** all three game *servers* register with notify, but only
  wordgame has the **client half** (`games/wordgame/src/notify/`:
  `playerKey`, `api`, `useNotifyBind`, the settings panel). Acquire and
  Rail Baron players never bind, so they never enter anyone's ledger and
  can never be invited by name. That is the prerequisite below, owed for
  Acquire now.
- **Timing, which is a feature:** bind happens at play phase, not in the
  lobby. The ledger records people you actually played a game with, not
  people who looked into a lobby and left.

Two gaps stand between that definition and a feature: the history
evaporates (`roomRemoved` deletes the room record, so nothing can be
derived on demand), and notify knows no names.

## Prerequisite: the notify client becomes shared

Move `games/wordgame/src/notify/` (minus the game's own settings styling)
to `packages/notify/client/`, mirroring how `@game-host/lobby` splits
`client/` from `server/`. Wordgame re-imports it; Acquire adds the one
`useNotifyBind` call and a settings entry point (Rail Baron and Marco
Polo the same, when they come into scope; the UI checklist rules them
deferred for now). The PWA spec
does the analogous extraction for the service worker and expects to meet
push enrollment at the key-landing moment; that enrollment prompt is
notify client code and should land in the same package. Until this is
done, everything below is a wordgame-only feature and should be planned
as such.

## The design

Five pieces. The ledger (2) is the substance; the rest feeds it a name
(1), reads it out safely (3), spends it (4), or reserves its future (5).

### 1. Profiles get a display name

`ProfileRecord` grows an optional `name`. The client stamps it onto the
existing bind call (`POST /notify/bind` grows a `name` field), taken from
the room identity the bind hook already holds (`identity.name`, the name
used in *that* room, with the shared `lobby.name` as fallback). Last-
writer-wins, trimmed, length-capped, same guard discipline as every other
record field. The alternative, a contract capability asking games for
roster names, was considered and declined: more plumbing across the host
boundary for a name that dies with the room, when the client already
holds it and is already making the call.

The name is what other players' pickers show. It is exactly as public as
sitting down at the table was, and it refreshes on every bind, so a
rename propagates the next time its owner plays.

### 2. The co-player ledger

When `bindSeat` binds a profile into a room, notify writes reciprocal
**contact entries** between the binder and every *other seat* already
bound there. Claiming an invite runs the same bind path, so invited
players enter the ledger too. Both directions are written at the later
bind, when both sides are known.

**The unit of a contact is a seat's worth of profiles, not one profile.**
The parent spec makes `bindings[playerId]` a set: two devices on one seat
are two profiles, and so is one person after a cleared browser or an iOS
storage-bucket split. The ledger treats every profile that has shared a
seat as one person:

```
ContactRecord {
  contactId,      // random, minted per owner; see section 3
  profileIds,     // every profile seen on that person's seats; internal, never leaves the server
  name,           // their profile name as of the last shared bind
  gameId,         // where we last played
  lastRoom,       // `${gameId}--${roomId}` of the last shared room; the no-op guard
  lastPlayedAt,
  status,         // 'played' | 'pending' | 'accepted' | 'blocked'; see section 5
}
```

Rules, in the order they matter:

- **Same-seat profiles never pair.** For the binder's seat, every profile
  bound to it is "me"; contact entries are written only toward other
  seats. Without this rule your own phone appears in your friend list the
  first time you redeem a seat key on it.
- **Merge by overlap.** For each other seat, take its bound profiles as
  "them". On each of my profiles, find an existing entry sharing any
  `profileId` with "them" and union the sets; otherwise create one.
  Reciprocally for each of theirs toward mine. Entries converge as people
  are seen together on more devices, which is the duplicate-person
  problem dissolving by construction, with no profile merging anywhere.
- **Re-binds are no-ops.** The bind hook is fire-and-forget on every
  mount, so a six-player room refreshing would otherwise rewrite six
  profiles per refresh. An entry is touched, and its owner's profile
  saved, only if `profileIds`, `name`, or `lastRoom` changed;
  `lastPlayedAt` bumps only when `lastRoom` does. One game together is
  one write per pair, however many refreshes it takes.
- **Capped.** Entries live on the owning `ProfileRecord`
  (`contacts: ContactRecord[]`), at most 100, oldest-`lastPlayedAt`
  evicted first. It is a "people I might invite" list, not an archive. A
  re-encounter after eviction recreates the entry with a fresh
  `contactId`, harmlessly.

### 3. Reading the list out

`POST /notify/contacts { playerKey }` returns, per entry: `contactId`,
`name`, `lastPlayedAt`, a game title, and `reachable`, a boolean meaning
"at least one enabled channel exists across any of that person's
profiles" (a confirmed email or a push subscription, prefs respected).
Never an address, never a `profileId`, and never *which* channel. The
game title comes from the live registration for `gameId`, falling back
to the bare `gameId` for a game that is no longer mounted rather than
failing the whole listing.

`contactId` is a per-owner random handle rather than a `profileId`,
deliberately: `profileId` is stable and global, so exposing it would let
any two players correlate their lists ("is your Alice my Alice?"), a
small leak but a free one to close. The server resolves a `contactId`
only within the caller's own ledger; someone else's, or a fabricated
one, gets the same shaped "no", mirroring the parent spec's non-probe
rule.

### 4. Invite by contact

The parent spec's invite endpoint grows a second target shape:

```
POST /notify/invite { playerKey, game, roomId, playerId, token, email }     // parent spec
POST /notify/invite { playerKey, game, roomId, playerId, token, contactId } // this spec
```

Everything up to delivery is the parent flow verbatim: the inviter
proves their own seat via `verifySeat`, notify asks the game to
`reserveSeat`, mints the invite token, records the invite.
`InviteRecord.email` becomes a discriminated target:
`{ kind: 'email', address }` or `{ kind: 'profile', profileIds }`, the
whole set from the contact entry, so that the invite reaches whichever
of that person's devices can be reached.

Delivery is where the shapes diverge. A profile-targeted invite fans out
across the target's profiles and their channels per prefs,
**immediately**: no debounce and no presence check, because those exist
to avoid notifying someone already looking at the room, and an invitee
has no room to be looking at. Email is deduped by address across the
profiles, as the parent spec's turn sends are. Push says "Pete invited
you to Acquire, room QZWXV" (the inviter's profile name, section 1) and
deep-links the room URL with the invite token; email is the parent
spec's invite mail to the already-confirmed address. A new payload kind
joins `TurnPayload` in `channels.ts`.

**Push routing for invites is the one exception to the PWA spec's
scope rule.** That spec tags each push subscription with the game whose
worker minted it and routes a game's turn notifications only to
matching-scope subscriptions, so a turn never opens inside another
game's app shell. Applied to invites, that rule would make a friend who
only ever installed wordgame unreachable by push for an Acquire invite,
with `reachable` saying otherwise. An invite is a doorway, not a turn:
it prefers subscriptions tagged with the target game, and **falls back
to any scope's subscription when none match**. Opening a wordgame room
from Acquire's installed shell is a mild smear; not being invited at all
is a failure. The PWA spec carries a sentence pointing here. This ruling
is also what keeps `reachable` a single boolean rather than one per game.

The link stays bearer, and claiming stays the parent spec's claim,
unchanged. The target profiles are who gets *told*, not a restriction on
who can claim; restricting claims would break exactly the multi-bucket
devices the seat key exists to serve.

No consent question arises that email invites had to answer: the
double-opt-in already happened when the address was confirmed, the
invite respects `prefs` and the unsubscribe state like any turn
notification, and section 5 reserves the harder social consent question
without answering it.

Refusals, all cheap and honest (the contact list is the caller's own
data, so unlike key redemption these are not probes), and all checked
**before** a seat is reserved so nothing dangles:

- `unreachable`: no enabled channel on any of the target's profiles.
- `alreadySeated`: one of the target's profiles is already bound to a
  seat in this room. They are in; there is nothing to invite them to.
- `blocked`: per section 5.
- `rateLimited`: see below.

Limits, indicative numbers, same counting shape as the email confirmation
limit: at most **3 invites per (inviter, target) per UTC day**, which is
the recipient's protection against a resend loop, and **20 per inviter
per UTC day** across both target shapes. At most one outstanding invite
per (room, target); a repeat within that is a resend, never a second
reservation. Both caps are soft: a profile is free to mint by clearing
storage. The real defense is structural and worth stating as such: by
name you can only ever invite people who chose to sit at a table with
you, and by address the parent spec's per-address limits hold.

### 5. The accept/reject stub, deliberately hollow

`status` on `ContactRecord` ships with one live value, `'played'`, and
three reserved ones: `'pending'`, `'accepted'`, `'blocked'`.

The v1 rule is that having shared a table is consent enough to be
invited back: a person you sat with can tap you on the shoulder. The
enum plus one enforcement point (invite-by-contact refuses a `'blocked'`
entry) is the entire stub. No request flow, no accept/reject UI, no
endpoints, no way to *reach* `'blocked'` yet. When friend requests earn
their keep, adding someone unplayed-with, consent-gating invites, muting
a specific person, they arrive as status transitions and endpoints over
this record, not as a schema change. Until then the invitee's real
defenses are the ones that already exist, channel prefs and unsubscribe,
plus the per-target cap above.

## UI this needs, and none of it exists yet (design handoff)

The per-game version of this list, organised by UI family with the
states each pattern takes, is
[2026-09-02-invites-ui-checklist.md](2026-09-02-invites-ui-checklist.md).

Everything below is a surface with **no current UI anywhere in the
repo**, listed so design can be briefed before planning. Items marked
*(parent)* are owed by the email-invites spec and shared with this one;
they are listed because a friend picker cannot ship without them.

1. **The invite picker**, in the lobby, and mid-game for a room with an
   open seat. One control with two ways in: a list of past co-players by
   name, and an email field *(parent)*. Each contact row shows the name,
   roughly when and in which game you last played, and its reachability.
   **Unreachable rows stay visible but cannot be picked**, with a reason
   a person can act on ("Sam hasn't turned on notifications"), because
   under the wider-than-it-sounds rule above most early entries will be
   unreachable and a list that hides them looks broken. Empty state for
   a player who has not played with anyone yet. Refusal states:
   already seated, daily limit reached.
2. **The reserved-seat row in the roster** *(parent)*: "invited, not
   yet here", named by whatever the inviter picked (the contact's name,
   or nothing for an email invite since the address must not show), with
   the host's **revoke** affordance. Also its mid-game form, since a
   pending seat is outside the game until claimed.
3. **The invite as received.** Push notification copy and the email
   template for an invite (a new mail beside the turn and confirmation
   mails), both carrying who invited you, to what, and one action.
4. **The landing moment after tapping the link** *(parent)*: the page
   loads, the client redeems the token, and the invitee needs to see that
   a seat was held for them and is now theirs, in the lobby and, harder,
   mid-game where they are slotting into a running rotation. The PWA spec
   wants push enrollment offered here too, so this screen has two jobs.
5. **Notification settings for Acquire.** Wordgame's
   `NotificationSettings` panel is the only one; the prerequisite makes
   the code shared but each game needs the entry point placed. Without
   it, nobody in Acquire can ever become reachable. (Rail Baron and
   Marco Polo have the same gap, deferred with them.)
6. **Sender identity.** The invite names the inviter by profile name,
   which is the last name they played under. There is no place to see or
   edit "the name others see me as" outside a room's own rename control;
   probably fine, but design should know the name comes from there.
7. **Not to design yet, on purpose:** friend requests, accept/reject,
   blocking, and any "friends" page outside the invite picker. The stub
   exists so these can arrive later without a schema change; giving them
   a surface now would imply a social layer this spec deliberately does
   not have.

## Testing

- Ledger: reciprocal entries written at bind, claim-path binds included;
  same-seat profiles never pair; overlap-merge unions `profileIds`;
  entries survive `roomRemoved`; a re-bind into the same room saves
  nothing; the cap evicts oldest-played first.
- Listing: the wire shape provably contains no address and no
  `profileId` (assert on serialized output, not intent); `reachable`
  tracks prefs and channel state across all of an entry's profiles;
  unmounted game falls back to `gameId`.
- Resolution: another profile's `contactId` and a fabricated one produce
  the same shaped refusal.
- Invite by contact: fans out through the fake channels per prefs across
  every profile in the entry, email deduped by address; sends immediately
  (no debounce timer involved); scope-preferred push with any-scope
  fallback; `unreachable`, `alreadySeated`, `blocked`, and `rateLimited`
  all refused before any seat is reserved; resend does not double-
  reserve; both caps.
- Wire tests still cannot prove possession properties (the CORS note in
  CLAUDE.md); assert on record state and response shapes, as the parent
  spec's tests do.

## Out of scope, deliberately

- **Friend requests and accept/reject.** The stub above, and nothing
  more, until something needs it.
- **Adding a friend you haven't played with.** No usernames, no search,
  no directory; that is an account system arriving sideways. The ledger
  grows by playing.
- **"Forget me."** No player can remove themselves from other people's
  ledgers, and nothing deletes a profile. Listed here rather than left
  silent: it is the first thing a privacy-minded player will ask for, and
  it would be a per-profile sweep inside notify when it comes.
- **Profile merging.** Inherited from the parent spec. The seat-based
  contact entry makes it matter far less for this feature, and keying
  everything off profiles is what keeps the eventual fix a migration.
- **Exposing which channel reaches a contact.** `reachable` is a
  boolean forever; "Sam has email but no push" is nobody's business.
- **Presence and social features.** No "online now", no profile pages,
  no counts of games played together. It is an invite picker, not a
  social graph.
